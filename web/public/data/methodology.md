# WaveScout Detection Methodology

*Updated: 2026-04-17*  
*How the evidence works, what it can support, and where it can fail.*

## Short Version

WaveScout looks for coastline locations that repeatedly show evidence of wave breaking in satellite imagery, then combines that evidence with coastline geometry, coarse bathymetry, and ocean context.

It is an evidence system, not a guarantee system.

WaveScout can support statements like:

- `this coastline segment repeatedly shows breaking-wave evidence`
- `this segment appears more active in some swell windows than others`

WaveScout cannot honestly support statements like:

- `this is definitely a good surf spot`
- `this spot is working right now`
- `this spot only works on exact tide X`

## Evidence Layers

### 1. Coastline Geometry

WaveScout first scores coastline segments on broad physical context, including:

- orientation and swell exposure
- headland, bay, and coastline shape
- coarse bathymetric context
- practical access context where available

Geometry is useful because it is stable and cheap to compute, but geometry alone is not enough to prove that a location is surfable.

### 2. Satellite Observations

WaveScout then looks at clear Sentinel-2 scenes for promising locations.

Why NIR matters:

- water absorbs near-infrared and tends to appear dark
- foam and whitewater reflect near-infrared and tend to appear bright

This makes NIR useful for seeing where wave-breaking evidence is concentrated, especially when compared across multiple scenes.

### 3. Ocean Context

For each observation, WaveScout joins coarse marine and weather context near the image timestamp.

This context is useful for broad correlation:

- swell height
- swell direction
- swell period
- wind context

This context is not accurate enough to justify exact local tide or break-quality claims.

## What Counts as Strong Evidence

Evidence gets stronger when a location shows several of these behaviors:

- repeated activity in clear scenes
- activity concentrated in a plausible breaking zone rather than spread uniformly along cliffs
- response that varies by swell size or direction
- geometry that supports the observed pattern

Evidence gets weaker when a location shows:

- uniform foam along rocky cliff faces
- activity only in contaminated scenes
- no clear pattern across conditions
- weak or contradictory geometry support

## Major Failure Modes

These are known product risks, not edge cases:

- clouds and haze
- snow and bright land surfaces
- cloud shadows
- swath-edge artifacts
- exposed wet sand at low tide
- foam generated by cliffs, rocks, and harbor structures

The product must surface these risks rather than hiding them.

## How Users Should Read the Product

### Surf Potential Score

This answers:

- how promising does this location look overall

### Evidence Confidence

This answers:

- how strong is the supporting data for that judgment

Users should treat:

- high potential + low confidence as a lead worth checking carefully
- moderate potential + high confidence as a credible but limited claim
- low confidence results as exploratory, not dependable

## Public Output Policy

WaveScout should bias toward coarse candidate presentation:

- confirmed public spots can be named directly
- unverified discoveries should be shown as candidate segments or sections
- precision should be reduced where disclosure is sensitive

## Data Sources

- Sentinel-2 imagery via Google Earth Engine
- Open-Meteo marine and weather context
- coarse bathymetry such as GEBCO
- coastline and road geometry
- known Nova Scotia spot references for calibration

## Bottom Line

WaveScout is best used as a structured coastal search tool. It helps users narrow the coastline and inspect evidence. It does not replace local knowledge, safety judgment, or actual observation in the water.
